Someone who spends five minutes designing a mug is far more interested than someone who glanced at a product page. When they leave without ordering, a short email sequence can bring many of them back.

## Email one: a friendly reminder

Send it within a few hours. Keep it short: the product, a line about finishing the design, and a button back to the store.

## Email two: answer the hesitation

A day later, answer the usual questions: delivery time, how the print will look, what happens if they need help. Doubt, not price, is the most common reason people leave.

## Email three: a gentle nudge

After a few days, a final note. If you offer it, a small incentive works here. Then stop: three emails are helpful, five are annoying.

## Keep it personal

- Use the product name, not "your cart"
- Show a product photo
- Make the reply-to address a real inbox

## Common mistakes

- **Generic subject lines.** "You left something behind" is everywhere. Mention the product instead.
- **Leading with a discount.** A discount in the first email teaches shoppers to leave and wait. Save it for the last one, if you use it at all.
- **No way to reply.** Many people leave because of a question. A reply-to address that reaches a person turns questions into orders.

## Measure what matters

Track how many people return to the store from each email and how many of them order. If the first email does most of the work, keep the sequence short. If the second email, the one that answers doubts, performs best, spend more time on its content. Review the emails each season: delivery times, prices and popular products change, and the emails should change with them.

## In short

Three short, personal emails, sent within days of the visit, bring back many shoppers who started a design. Lead with the product, answer the usual doubts, keep any incentive for the end, and make sure replies reach a real person who can help.
