Quantity discounts are one of the clearest ways to win bigger orders. Set them carelessly, though, and the biggest orders become the least profitable.

## Find where your cost drops

Setup time is spread over more items in a larger order, so the cost per item falls. Put your tiers where that happens, not at round numbers that feel generous.

## Decide what the discount applies to

You can discount the base product, the customization, or add-on options. Discounting only the base product keeps print and add-on margins intact on large orders.

## Fewer tiers, clearer choice

Three or four tiers are easy to understand. Ten tiers look like a negotiation. A short table on the product page lets buyers see the saving and move up a tier on their own.

## Common mistakes

- **Discounting everything.** If the discount also applies to every add-on, a large order with many extras can end up below cost.
- **Too many tiers.** Buyers spend time working out the best deal instead of ordering. Keep the steps simple.
- **Hiding the table.** A discount nobody sees does not change behaviour. Show the tiers on the product page.

## A simple tier structure

A pattern that works for many custom stores: full price for one to nine items, a modest discount from ten, a bigger one from fifty, and quotes for anything above a few hundred. Check each tier against your cost sheet so the largest tier still makes money after setup, printing and packing. Revisit the tiers once a year, or whenever a supplier changes prices, and tell repeat buyers when the table changes so there are no surprises on their next order.

## In short

Put tiers where your costs actually drop, choose carefully what the discount applies to, keep the table short and show it on the product page. Bulk buyers get a clear reason to order more, and your largest orders stay worth producing.
