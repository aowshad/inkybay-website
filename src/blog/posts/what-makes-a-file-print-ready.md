Most reprints have nothing to do with the printer. They start with a file that looked fine on a screen and was never going to look fine on a hoodie. This guide walks through the checks print shops run on every custom order, so you can catch problems before they reach production.

## Resolution: pixels at the size you print

A screen shows a design at around 72 to 150 pixels per inch. A printer needs far more detail, because the design is spread across a physical surface. The number that matters is **pixels per inch at print size** (PPI).

### How much resolution is enough?

| Print size | Minimum pixels | Good result at |
|---|---|---|
| Chest logo, 4 in wide | 600 px wide | 1200 px wide |
| Full front, 12 in wide | 1800 px wide | 3600 px wide |
| Mug wrap, 8.5 in wide | 1275 px wide | 2550 px wide |
| Poster, 18 in wide | 2700 px wide | 5400 px wide |

The minimum column works at 150 PPI, which is fine for most apparel. The right column is 300 PPI, which keeps small text and fine lines sharp on hard goods like mugs and phone cases.

### Vector beats pixels when it can

Logos, text and flat illustrations print best as vector files (SVG, PDF or EPS). They are drawn with shapes instead of pixels, so they stay sharp at any size. Photos are always pixels, so for photo products the only fix for a small file is a bigger original.

## Colour: what you see is not what you print

Screens mix red, green and blue light (RGB). Most printing mixes cyan, magenta, yellow and black inks (CMYK), or uses spot inks for screen printing and thread for embroidery. Some bright RGB colours, especially neon greens and electric blues, simply do not exist in ink.

- Expect very saturated colours to print slightly duller.
- Dark colours on dark garments need a white underbase, or they disappear.
- For brand colours, ask your printer for a swatch print before a large run.

> [!NOTE]
> The thing customers do not know: a design that looks bright on a phone in a dark room can look flat on fabric in daylight. Showing a realistic preview, on the actual product colour, sets the right expectation before they order.

## Safe areas, bleed and seams

Every product has edges that the printer cannot hit exactly. Keep important parts of the design inside the **safe area**, and extend background colour past the edge into the **bleed** so no white gap shows after trimming. On garments, avoid seams, collars and zips: text that runs into a seam will never print straight.

> "We used to fix files by hand every morning. Once shoppers could only place designs inside the print area, the morning fixes stopped."

## File types that print well

1. **PDF or SVG** for vector artwork, logos and text.
2. **PNG** with a transparent background for artwork placed on a product colour.
3. **High-quality JPG** only for full photo prints, saved at the highest quality setting.

Avoid screenshots, images copied from websites and files that were enlarged after the fact. Enlarging adds pixels but no detail.

## A pre-production checklist

- [x] Artwork is at least 150 PPI at print size (300 PPI for hard goods)
- [x] Text and key details sit inside the safe area
- [x] Background colour extends into the bleed
- [x] Fonts are outlined or embedded
- [x] Colours were checked against the product colour
- [x] Transparent PNG for anything placed on a garment

## FAQ

### Can I just enlarge a small image?

You can, but it will look soft or blocky. Enlarging creates new pixels from the old ones without adding real detail. Ask the customer for the original photo or a vector logo instead.

### Why does my printer ask for a white underbase?

On dark garments, inks are partly see-through. A white layer underneath keeps colours bright. Your printer adds it during production; you only need to know that it changes the cost and the feel of the print.

### What resolution should product mockups be?

Mockups are for screens, so 1500 to 2000 pixels on the long side is plenty. Keep the print file separate and at full print resolution.
